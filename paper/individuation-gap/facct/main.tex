\title{Who Can Be Read? An Accountability Framework and Claims Audit for
Individual Neuro-Inference}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Products and clinical tools increasingly claim to infer an individual's emotion, attention, or likely
behaviour from brain and body signals, and increasingly from a \emph{predicted} brain, where a model maps a
stimulus to cortical activity that is then read as a measurement. These claims are sold to advertisers,
employers, and clinicians, and they assert private knowledge about specific people, yet there is no shared
standard for deciding which of them can be true. We supply one. We distinguish three claim types,
population, identity, and individual content, and show they have sharply different evidentiary status: a
simple and provable boundary, the individuation gap, makes individual-content claims from a zero-shot
predicted brain unsupportable, while identity and population claims are supportable under stated tests. On
this boundary we build three accountability artifacts. An audit rubric classifies a marketed claim and
assigns it a test and a verdict without access to the vendor's model. A regulatory mapping places the three
claim types against emotion-recognition and biometric-categorization rules, data-protection law, and
consumer-protection enforcement, and shows that the claims least supportable by evidence are also the ones
regulation most restricts. A disclosure standard and a refusal-first deployment pattern keep identity
recognition, which works, separate from mental-content reading, which does not. The contribution is a
concrete and falsifiable way to hold individual neuro-inference claims accountable, grounded in a verifiable
technical boundary rather than in contested notions of harm.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{neurotechnology, individual inference, accountability, transparency, auditing, emotion

\section{Introduction}
Systems that infer facts about people are the core concern of fairness, accountability, and transparency, and
a fast-growing class of them infers mental content. Affective-computing products score emotion from a face,
a voice, or a wearable; brand-intelligence platforms sell predicted audience response; and a new wave of
neuro-mental-health tools offers to read an individual's state from brain or body signals. A growing share of
these systems does not measure a brain at all. Instead a model maps a stimulus or context to a predicted
pattern of cortical activity, which is then labelled with construct names, attention, reward, stress, intent,
and reported as if it had been recorded \cite{poldrack2006,hutzler2013,yao2024}. The label does the selling,
and the label asserts knowledge about a specific person.

The accountability problem is that these individual claims are asserted, priced, and acted on before anyone
has agreed how to tell which are true. A job applicant screened by an emotion-recognition tool cannot contest
the inference that they lacked enthusiasm. A shopper profiled by a predicted-brain platform cannot see, let
alone rebut, the claim that an advertisement moved their reward system. A patient offered a personalized
neuro-readout cannot easily distinguish a validated measurement from a marketing construct. In each case the
operator holds what looks like privileged, scientific knowledge about the person, and the person and the
public hold no agreed means to check it. This is an information asymmetry of a particular kind: not merely
that the operator knows more, but that the operator's claim is framed as a neural measurement, which borrows
the authority of neuroscience while evading its standards of evidence.

This paper gives a means to check the claim. Our central move is to stop treating ``reading the brain'' as a
single capability and to separate three kinds of claim that are routinely conflated. A \emph{population}
claim is about an aggregate, an audience, or a market, and not about any one person. An \emph{identity} claim
recognizes or verifies which person is present. An \emph{individual-content} claim states what a specific
person feels, attends to, or will choose. These three are not interchangeable, and they do not share an
evidentiary status. A short and verifiable technical boundary, which we call the individuation gap, makes
individual-content claims from a predicted brain that has never seen the person unsupportable, while identity
and population claims are supportable under tests we state.

On this boundary we build the accountability machinery that the field asks for but that neuro-inference
claims currently escape. We contribute (1) a taxonomy of individual neuro-inference claims, with the test
that would support each type and its current evidentiary status; (2) an audit rubric that takes a marketed
claim to a verdict without access to the vendor's model (Figure~\ref{fig:audit}); (3) a worked audit of the
claim categories that dominate the market; (4) a mapping of the taxonomy onto emotion-recognition,
biometric-categorization, data-protection, and consumer-protection regimes (Table~\ref{tab:reg}), which shows
that evidence and law already point the same way; and (5) a disclosure standard and a refusal-first
deployment pattern that keep identity separate from content. Our aim is not to adjudicate whether
neurotechnology is good or bad, but to give regulators, auditors, and builders a concrete and falsifiable
basis for deciding which individual claims may be made.

\section{Related work}
Our work sits at the meeting point of three literatures. The first is \emph{algorithmic auditing and
accountability}. External and internal audits have exposed the gap between what systems claim and what they
deliver, most influentially for facial analysis \cite{buolamwini2018}, and have proposed process frameworks
to close the gap through documentation and impact assessment \cite{raji2020,metcalf2021}. These frameworks
ask whether a system works as claimed and who is accountable when it does not; they do not, on their own,
supply the domain-specific test that decides whether a \emph{neural} claim about an individual can be true.
We contribute that test for the neuro-inference setting and fold it into an audit an outsider can run.

The second is the \emph{critique of inference about inner states}. A large body of work questions whether
affect and intent can be read from external signals at the individual level: emotion cannot be reliably
inferred from facial configuration \cite{barrett2019}, and inferring a cognitive process from a brain region
is unreliable because regions are not specific to processes \cite{poldrack2006,hutzler2013}. Our
contribution is to convert this general caution into a sharp, provable boundary for the specific and
commercially active case of a \emph{predicted} brain, and to separate the part of the claim that does hold
(identity) from the part that does not (content).

The third is \emph{neurorights and the governance of neurotechnology}, which argues for new protections such
as mental privacy and cognitive liberty \cite{ienca2017}, and the emerging hard law that already touches
these systems: the European Union's rules on emotion recognition and biometric categorization
\cite{euaiact2024}, data-protection law's treatment of biometric and health data \cite{gdpr2016}, biometric
privacy statutes \cite{bipa2008}, and consumer-protection doctrine on deceptive efficacy claims. Prior work
in this strand makes the normative case; we add the operational link, showing that the claim type the
evidence cannot support is the same claim type the law most restricts, and giving auditors a rubric that
connects the two.

\section{Background: the individuation gap}
We state the boundary compactly; it is elementary and is proved, with machine-checked support, in a companion
technical analysis. Let an outcome be $Y=S+\varepsilon$ with idiosyncratic-noise variance $v>0$, and let a
predictor resolve an identifiable \emph{between-person} signal variance $a\ge 0$. The best-case squared
correlation between predictor and outcome is $\rho^2 = a/(a+v)$. Averaging over $N$ people replaces $v$ with
$v/N$ and so raises $\rho^2$; this is the population regime, and it is why aggregate neural signals can
forecast markets and audiences even when the sampled people's own choices do not
\cite{knutson2007,dmochowski2014}. A zero-shot predicted brain, by contrast, emits the same features for every
unseen person, so it carries no between-person variance, $a=0$, and $\rho^2=0$. The individual signal is not
merely hard to extract; it is absent from the input, so no model and no amount of pooling recovers it.

Per-person enrollment can supply $a>0$, but on current encoders and signals that enrollment recovers a
person's \emph{identity} far more readily than their \emph{behaviour}. In-scanner fingerprinting from
subject-specific encoders reaches 100\% identification, and a behavioural fingerprint reaches many times
chance, while the direct link from an individual's neural signal to that individual's behaviour abstains under
a calibrated test. The measurement literature points the same way: individual brain-behaviour effects are
small and need very large samples \cite{marek2022,kang2024}, individual test-retest reliability of common
measures is low \cite{elliott2020}, and individual precision mapping takes hours of data per person
\cite{gordon2017}. These facts are the premise of this paper, not its contribution; what follows builds
accountability on top of them.

\section{A taxonomy of individual neuro-inference claims}
We separate three claim types by what they assert about a person, because the evidentiary burden and the
regulatory exposure differ by type.

\paragraph{Population claims.} The claim is about an aggregate, an audience, or a market, and not about any
one person. ``This advertisement will raise purchase intent in the target segment'' is a population claim. It
is supportable when the predicted index beats a content-only baseline out of sample and carries a calibrated
interval, and the baseline matters because a predicted neural index is a function of the stimulus and can
reproduce what the stimulus already predicts while adding nothing of its own. Population claims are the
regime where neuroforecasting has genuine support.

\paragraph{Identity claims.} The claim recognizes or verifies which person is present, for example that a
pattern of responses belongs to enrolled user A rather than user B. It is supportable when a fingerprint
exceeds chance on held-out data, which it reliably does. Precisely because it works, it is a biometric
capability, and it carries the re-identification and surveillance risks that attach to biometrics; a system
that can tell people apart can also track them.

\paragraph{Individual-content claims.} The claim states what a specific person feels, attends to, or will
choose: ``this applicant is anxious,'' ``this user wants the product,'' ``this voter will be moved.'' From a
zero-shot predicted brain, an individual-content claim is unsupportable by the individuation gap. With
enrollment it becomes possible in principle, but on current systems it is modest, data-hungry, and must be
shown to beat a population model for that specific person before it earns the label.

The three types are routinely conflated, and the conflation is where accountability fails. A system that
fingerprints a person well (identity) is advertised as reading that person's mind (content). A system that
forecasts an audience well (population) is sold as reading the individuals who compose it. The taxonomy makes
the substitution visible, and because each type has an explicit test, it makes the substitution checkable.

\section{An audit rubric}
Figure~\ref{fig:audit} turns the taxonomy into a procedure an auditor can run on a product claim without
access to the vendor's model or training data. The procedure has four steps. First, \emph{classify}: read the
marketed claim and decide what it asserts about the person, population, identity, or individual content,
using the claim's own language and its intended use. Second, \emph{apply the test} for that type: a
content-baseline and calibration test for population claims, a held-out fingerprinting test above chance for
identity claims, and for individual-content claims a demonstration that an individual's neural signal predicts
that individual's behaviour better than a population model, which the individuation gap shows is impossible
zero-shot and demanding under enrollment. Third, \emph{record the verdict and the required safeguards};
identity claims that pass still inherit biometric obligations. Fourth, \emph{default to unsupported}: a claim
that cannot be assigned a passing test is recorded as unsupported, so the burden of evidence rests on the
operator rather than on the person being scored.

Two properties make the rubric usable in practice. It is \emph{black-box}: it classifies and tests the
claim, not the internals of the model, so it can be applied to a product description, a marketing page, or a
procurement document. And it \emph{separates the channels}: a vendor can be credited for a working identity
or population capability without being credited for an unsupported content capability, which prevents a real
capability in one channel from laundering an unsupported claim in another.

\section{A worked audit of market claim categories}
We apply the rubric to the claim categories that dominate the market. We audit \emph{categories} of claim
rather than named products, both to keep the analysis general and to avoid singling out firms; the point is
structural.

\emph{Neuromarketing audience scoring} typically reports how a segment will respond to creative. Classified,
this is a population claim; tested, it can pass if the vendor shows the predicted index beats a content-only
baseline and carries an interval. The accountability failure here is not the capability but the slippage when
the same score is re-described as reading an individual viewer.

\emph{Affective-computing state detection} reports an individual's current emotion from face, voice, or
wearable signals. Classified, this is an individual-content claim. Tested, it must beat a population model for
the specific person and rest on a construct with a validated localizer; on the evidence, individual emotion
read-out at this level is not supportable, which aligns with the broader finding that emotion cannot be
reliably inferred from external configuration \cite{barrett2019}. The rubric records it as unsupported absent
per-person evidence.

\emph{Personalized neuro-wellness read-outs} offer an individual a number for stress, focus, or mood from a
predicted or low-channel signal. Classified, these are individual-content claims; tested, they face the
individuation gap when the system is zero-shot and the enrollment price when it is not. The honest version of
such a product reports a population-calibrated or identity-based function and says so.

\emph{Neural authentication} verifies a person from their responses. Classified, this is an identity claim;
tested, it can pass, and the rubric then routes it to biometric safeguards rather than to a mind-reading
claim. This is the one category where the capability and the claim coincide, and the accountability task is
to keep it from being marketed as content.

Across categories, the recurring pattern is that a working population or identity capability is re-described
as individual mind-reading. Naming which of the three a product actually delivers is most of the
accountability work, and the rubric makes that naming routine.

\section{Mapping to regulation}
The taxonomy aligns with where regulation already draws its lines (Table~\ref{tab:reg}). The European Union's
AI regulation restricts the use of emotion-recognition systems in workplaces and education and restricts
biometric categorization, which maps onto our individual-content and identity rows \cite{euaiact2024}.
Data-protection law treats biometric data used to identify a person, and data concerning health, as special
categories subject to heightened conditions, which maps onto the identity row and onto health-adjacent
content claims \cite{gdpr2016}. Dedicated biometric-privacy statutes impose consent and disclosure
obligations on the collection of biometric identifiers, again tracking the identity row \cite{bipa2008}. And
consumer-protection doctrine reaches deceptive efficacy claims, which is precisely what an unsupported
individual-content claim is.

The alignment is not a coincidence. The claim that the evidence least supports, individual content from a
predicted brain, is also the claim that most directly asserts private knowledge about a person's inner state,
which is what these regimes are built to guard. A practical implication follows for each stakeholder. For
regulators, the taxonomy gives a technical criterion to attach to emotion-recognition and biometric rules,
so that enforcement can distinguish a supportable identity capability from an unsupportable content claim.
For procurement, it gives a question to ask of any vendor: which of the three claim types is this, and which
test did it pass. For auditors, it gives the black-box rubric above.

\section{A disclosure standard and a refusal-first pattern}
Accountability needs an artifact that survives deployment, not only an audit performed once. We propose two.

A \emph{disclosure standard} requires an operator to attach to each reported score three fields: the claim
type (population, identity, or individual content), the test the score passed, and the uncertainty it
carries. A score that cannot name its type or its test is disclosed as unsupported rather than presented as a
measurement. The standard is deliberately lightweight, so it can live on a results screen, in an API
response, or in a datasheet, and it is checkable by the same rubric an auditor uses. Its effect is to move the
default from assert-by-default to disclose-by-default, and to make the identity-for-content substitution a
visible misstatement rather than an ambiguous one.

A \emph{refusal-first} deployment pattern builds the standard into the system. An individual-content score is
returned only when the construct has a validated localizer, the predicted index beats a content-only
baseline, and the score carries a conformal interval; otherwise the system returns nothing for that channel.
Identity and content are computed and governed on separate channels, so a system may verify who a person is
without asserting that it has read their mind, and the biometric safeguards that attach to identity do not
leak permission to make content claims. The pattern does not forbid individual-content claims forever; it
requires that they be earned, per person, by evidence, which is the posture the individuation gap and the
reliability literature jointly warrant.

\section{Discussion and broader impact}
The framework has a direction. It makes it harder to sell individual mind-reading that the evidence does not
support, and easier to credit the population and identity capabilities that it does. The protective impact is
the main one: a person scored by such a system gains a basis to contest the claim, and a regulator gains a
technical criterion to attach to existing rules. There is a countervailing risk. The identity capability,
which we show is the part that works, is a biometric one, and a framework that validates it could be read as
licensing re-identification or surveillance. We therefore treat identity as a restricted, separately-governed
channel throughout, and we recommend that a passing identity test trigger biometric safeguards by default
rather than commercial reuse. A second risk is that the audit's black-box simplicity invites gaming, where a
vendor passes a population or identity test and then makes content claims off-channel; the disclosure standard
exists to catch exactly this.

\section{Limitations}
The boundary we build on is a best-case abstraction; richer encoders change its constants but not its sign,
since a zero-shot predictor carries no between-person variance. The regulatory mapping is a structural
alignment rather than legal advice, and specific obligations vary by jurisdiction and change over time. The
audit rubric classifies claims, not internal model quality, so it must be paired with the disclosure standard
to catch off-channel claims. The worked audit addresses claim categories rather than named products, which
keeps it general but means a given product must still be audited on its own terms. The empirical figures
quoted as premises come from existing public datasets and the cited literature, and the companion technical
analysis should be consulted for their derivation.
